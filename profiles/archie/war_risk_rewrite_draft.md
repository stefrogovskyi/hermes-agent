Shipping lanes shift with politics before they shift with tides. That's roughly the situation shippers are working inside as 2026 unfolds: sanctions frameworks keep evolving, certain corridors stay volatile for months at a stretch, and routing decisions that used to be purely operational now carry real financial weight. Marine cargo insurance handles the basics: loss, damage, the ordinary mishaps of moving goods across oceans. War is usually a different conversation entirely, one that standard policies leave out unless someone specifically asks for it.

This piece by SeaRates walks through what war risk insurance covers, when it's worth buying, how insurers land on a price, and where shippers tend to get tripped up when a claim actually needs to be filed.

## When War Risk Cover Actually Matters

Not every shipment needs it. A container moving between two stable ports on a well-worn trade lane probably doesn't. The calculation changes once exposure moves past normal transit risk, and in 2026 even a routing choice made purely for cost or schedule reasons can quietly raise that exposure.

Buying war risk cover starts to make sense once cargo is routed through conflict-adjacent regions, through multiple port calls or transhipment hubs with questionable stability, or into jurisdictions flagged for sanctions or restricted trade. High-value cargo that can't easily be replaced belongs in this category too, as do shipments running against tight, non-negotiable delivery windows.

None of this is purely the shipper's call, either. Banks attach war risk conditions to Letters of Credit. Buyers and sellers inherit responsibility depending on which Incoterm governs the deal. Carriers, by choosing one route over another, can shift what coverage is actually needed without anyone updating the paperwork. There are operational tells worth watching for on top of the contractual ones: transit near active conflict, port calls in places with a shaky track record, longer than usual dwell time at terminals, cargo sitting in storage longer than planned. ## What "War Risk" Really Means on Paper

War risk isn't a standardized product the way, say, a basic liability policy might be. Two insurers can write "war risk" cover with meaningfully different scope, and the wording is what actually decides what a claim recovers, not the label on the policy.

Coverage typically extends to war and armed conflict, and sometimes to strikes or civil unrest if that's written in. Seizure or detention of cargo tends to be included, along with physical dangers like mines or torpedoes. Terrorism is a separate question: it needs to be named explicitly in the policy, because insurers don't treat it as automatically bundled into "war."

The exclusions matter just as much, arguably more, since they're what actually kills a claim after the fact. Sanctions or illegality clauses tied to restricted trade can void cover outright. Nuclear, chemical, and biological contamination sits outside most policies by default. Cyber and electronic data exclusions have also become fairly standard. None of this is uniform across insurers, so treating any of it as a given based on a general policy label is a mistake worth avoiding.

## Marine Cargo Insurance Won't Save You Here

A lot of shippers assume conflict risk rides along inside their existing cargo policy. It usually doesn't. Marine cargo insurance forms the base layer, the everyday protection against loss and damage in transit, while war-related exposure sits in its own category and typically gets excluded unless someone deliberately adds it back in.

"All risk" is a particularly misleading label here. It sounds comprehensive, but war perils are excluded by default under most all risk wording, and getting them included means adding an endorsement or buying a standalone policy. The policy language decides the outcome, not the marketing name printed on the cover page.

So the practical question every shipper should be asking is simple: is war risk already sitting inside your cargo policy, or do you need to go add it separately? From there it's a choice between three paths: layering war risk onto the existing cargo policy as an endorsement, buying standalone war risk insurance (sometimes structured as contingent war risk insurance, which activates when the primary responsible party's cover fails to respond), or blending both depending on how exposed a given route actually is. Frequency of shipping, cost tolerance, and how dangerous the lane is in practice all factor into which path makes sense.

## Why Premiums Move the Way They Do

There's no fixed tariff here. War risk pricing tracks real-world exposure and moves as the political and operational picture shifts underneath it, sometimes within days.

Underwriters look at lane and regional risk classification (areas flagged by bodies like the Joint War Committee tend to draw closer scrutiny and higher quotes), port calls and transhipment exposure, the flag and ownership behind the vessel, cargo type and value and how it's packaged, and how long the exposure actually lasts once dwell time at ports gets factored in. The Red Sea has been the clearest recent illustration of how fast this can move: when a corridor's risk classification shifts, the war risk premium attached to ships and cargo passing through it can shift right along with it, sometimes showing up on carrier invoices as a separate war risk surcharge.

Routing decisions feed straight back into cost. Shippers who can avoid the highest-risk maritime corridors, cut unnecessary transhipment stops, or reduce dwell time in exposed ports are managing their insurance cost as much as their insurance risk. Freight rerouting away from a flagged corridor is often the single biggest lever available. ## Building Coverage Without Gaps

Coverage gaps rarely come from bad insurance. They come from a mismatch between what the policy assumes and what actually happens on the ground: shipment data that's slightly off, contractual responsibility nobody nailed down, transit conditions that shifted after the policy was bound.

A structured process closes most of that gap. Map the full route first, ports, transhipment points, and any storage locations along the way. Pin down who the insured party actually is and confirm where responsibility sits under the relevant Incoterm. Run sanctions screening and restricted party checks before anything moves. Match the sum insured to the correct valuation basis for the cargo. Make sure warehouse-to-warehouse coverage applies where it needs to. And confirm, in writing, what documentation the insurer will want if a claim ever needs to be filed, before the shipment leaves the dock. ## The Paperwork That Actually Gets Claims Paid

Claims move fast or slow largely based on the quality of what gets submitted, not the merits of the underlying loss. Most delays trace back to incomplete or inconsistent records rather than any real dispute about whether the incident happened.

The usual documentation set: commercial invoice and packing list, bill of lading or air waybill, a survey or incident report, photographic evidence, tracking logs and exception reports, proof of cargo value, and evidence of whatever mitigation steps were taken at the time. Insurers want to see all of it lining up.

Friction tends to show up in a few predictable spots. Documentation that doesn't match the insured valuation basis. Shipment records that contradict each other across different documents. Incident reports filed late or filled in halfway. Getting policy terms and operational paperwork aligned before anything goes wrong is worth more than any amount of negotiating after a claim gets denied.

## A Few Words Before You Book

Geopolitical risk in shipping isn't going away in 2026, and pretending a shipment is "probably fine" because it's always been fine is how gaps happen. War risk insurance works when it's bought deliberately, matched to the actual route, and backed by paperwork that holds up under scrutiny. Buying the policy is step one. Making it fit the shipment is the part that actually protects the cargo.

## Shipper Checklist and Questions Worth Asking Your Broker

Before binding coverage, most shippers benefit from running through a fixed list rather than relying on memory: full route mapped, high-risk regions identified, war risk inclusion confirmed, insured party defined, Incoterms verified, sanctions screening completed, cargo value accurately declared, policy wording reviewed line by line, coverage period aligned with the actual voyage, documentation requirements confirmed in advance, route-specific risk reviewed one more time, and coverage gaps checked before the shipment actually leaves.

Worth raising directly with a broker: Is war risk included or excluded in this policy? What perils, specifically, are covered? Do any sanctions clauses apply to this route? What documents will be required if a claim gets filed? How is pricing determined for this particular lane?

## FAQs

**Is war risk insurance mandatory?**
Not universally. It can be required under contracts, financing terms, or because of how high-risk a route is.

**Does marine cargo insurance include war risk?**
Usually not. War-related risks are commonly excluded unless they're added specifically.

**What routes typically require war risk cover?**
Routes through conflict zones, politically unstable regions, or maritime corridors flagged as high-risk.

**Is terrorism covered under war risk?**
Sometimes, depending entirely on the policy wording and how the insurer defines it.

**How is the war risk premium calculated?**
Based on route exposure, cargo value, vessel-related factors, and how long the transit lasts. There's no single formula that applies across the board.
