# Article

Freight moves on paper as much as it moves on wheels, and somebody has to keep both moving at the same pace.

Dispatchers juggle drivers, load confirmations, and a phone that never really stops ringing. Add freight documentation and customers waiting on status updates, and the to-do list only grows. None of it is particularly hard work on its own. It's the volume that wears people down, and the way it eats into hours you'd rather spend on the parts of the business that actually grow it.

More freight companies are turning to virtual assistants for these functions instead of adding full-time staff. A properly trained VA can take on billing, documentation, carrier calls, and dispatch work, and the monthly cost typically lands well below what a full-time domestic employee runs once you count salary, benefits, and a desk.

The catch is that most VA services aren't built for this industry. A general VA can run your calendar and clear your inbox just fine, but hand them a load board or a bill of lading and watch the confusion set in. Industry knowledge matters more than a fast typing speed or perfect email etiquette.

This list only includes providers with real experience in trucking, freight forwarding, supply chain work, and logistics generally.

A dedicated account manager stays attached to the account for the length of the engagement, and if the VA turns out to be a poor fit, Wishup swaps them out at no extra cost. Best for logistics teams that need dependable back-office support more than specialized freight expertise. Think inbox management, calendar juggling, CRM updates, data entry, vendor emails, reporting, scheduling, and the kind of documentation support that keeps the paperwork side of the business from backing up.

## What to Look for Before You Commit

Hiring a VA for a logistics operation isn't quite the same exercise as hiring one to manage your calendar, and a few things are worth working through before you sign anything.

Industry background counts for more than general competence here. Someone who has touched a load board, a TMS platform, or a freight bill before will be useful in the first week. Someone without that background needs weeks just to learn the vocabulary, let alone the workflows.

Freight doesn't stop just because the clock hits 5 PM. Multi-region operations or after-hours needs mean confirming upfront that a provider can really deliver that kind of coverage on a sales call, and follow through on it once the contract is signed.

Dedicated versus shared assistants is worth asking about specifically. A dedicated VA learns your lanes, your carriers, your regular clients, over time, and that knowledge compounds. Bring in a new person every few months to rotate through the account, and you're back to square one each time.

And then there's the exit question, which people forget to ask until it's too late. A trial period or reasonable exit terms protect both sides if the match doesn't work out. It only takes a minute to check before signing anything.
