## How to detect congestion early (before it is recognized by the port)?

### Anchorage queue: the fastest indicator

When a port's throughput comes under pressure, vessels start piling up at anchor first. It's the earliest warning available, showing up two to four days before delays at the berth become public knowledge.

Four things worth checking daily: the number of vessels currently at anchor, the median waiting time, the P90 waiting time (this is the one that catches tail risk, the vessels sitting far longer than everyone else), and the queue growth rate, meaning how many extra vessels show up each day.

#### Tracking it in practice

You can spot the trend by watching individual vessel statuses move between "Underway," "At anchor," and "In port," then comparing the planned ETA against what actually happens. This is where real-time vessel tracking earns its keep: a Vessel Tracking tool gives shippers, cargo owners, and logistics providers exactly this kind of visibility, location, port calls, ETA history, down to one specific ship.

### Schedule reliability

Here the trend matters more than any single delay.

Beyond the obvious ETA miss, watch how often ETAs get revised (several changes packed into a few days point to something systemic), and whether calls get skipped or sailings go blank, since omitted calls and blank sailings both signal a network-level decision rather than a local hiccup. Add service rotation breakdowns to that list, cases where the same service keeps missing its planned port sequence.

An ETA that moves once and then holds is easy to absorb. Repeated shifts over several days are the real problem, because by then nothing downstream can be scheduled with confidence.

## Vessel diversions and what they cost you

Change the route, and delivery time grows almost immediately. Feeder connections shift out of sync, rail and road bookings stop lining up, and unloading at the warehouse slides down the calendar.

When several vessels from the same service reroute together, the delay doesn't stay isolated, it stacks across the whole line. Ships also tend to arrive at alternative ports in batches after a diversion rather than trickling in evenly, so new congestion builds on top of the old: the anchorage queue grows fast, berth waiting time increases, and the container yard fills up.

For your cargo specifically, a rotation change touches more than the sailing date. Your container arrives later, the transshipment port itself may change, and the feeder or rail connection built around the original schedule can break. Planning accuracy erodes as the queue at the roadstead grows and containers sit in port longer than budgeted for. There's also fresh risk at whatever hub the ship diverts to, and the economics shift too: Incoterms validity windows, storage costs, demurrage and detention fees, and insurance coverage can all depend on which route the cargo actually took.

### How Ship Schedules helps you read a diversion

When a ship changes course, the real question is whether that's a one-off deviation or the line restructuring its whole service. Answering that means looking past the ship's current position into the structure of the rotation itself.

The Route tab in Ship Schedules lets you check the full sequence of ports on a given route, see planned arrival dates port by port, track the interval between voyages on that route, and compare different carriers' services between two points to book the better tariff.

That's enough to catch the real signals of disruption: a port quietly dropped from rotation, calls happening out of order, an ETA shift running across the entire line rather than one stop, or a widening gap between vessels on the same service. What you're looking at, at that point, isn't one delayed ship. It's a trade line changing its logic.

## What this means for planning in March 2026

Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, COSCO, and ONE have publicly confirmed they're reviewing routes and pushing some services onto longer alternative passages. Some voyages have already diverted around Africa, stretching transit times between Asia and Europe automatically.

Extend a service like that and the entire rotation moves: port arrival windows shift, feeder connections break, rail and road slot schedules get disrupted, and vessel flow into ports turns uneven.

AIS data analytics capture this too. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has slowed by roughly 70%, with a number of vessels turning back or changing their declared destination port altogether. The planned ETA on a single voyage can get adjusted several times over, and downstream planning simply can't keep pace with that.

Marine insurers, meanwhile, have started revising or cancelling geopolitical risk coverage for certain transit areas. That changes voyage economics for shipowners and shows up in the market as higher freight rates and surcharges. Follow the chain: the route gets more expensive, the service gets revised, transit times stretch, and alternative ports absorb the extra volume.

The visible fallout so far: service regularity down on certain routes, wider intervals between ships, fewer departures to some regions, and higher transportation costs tied to fuel prices. Brent crude has also climbed around 9%, a sign that delivery disruptions and transit risk are reaching into global commodity supply chains more broadly.

## Supply planning specifics

Average transit time has stopped being the number that matters most. Production schedules and just-in-time delivery increasingly hinge on how late a vessel runs after being pushed onto an alternative route.

Cost risk concentrates at the connection points. Uneven arrivals at a hub mean containers sit idle longer in a congested port, and demurrage becomes a real possibility even inside a normal free-time window. That delay then rolls straight into land logistics and warehouse operations.

Insurance and contract terms deserve more attention too. If the route changed after booking and nobody rechecked the insurance coverage or the Incoterms, that risk lands on the cargo owner.

## Your next operational step for March 2026

Expectations aren't going to hold up in this market. Routes have gotten longer, rotations are being reshuffled, and insurance conditions are moving under everyone's feet.

Proactive planning breaks down into four steps.

Step 1: Move from static to dynamic control, the kind that gives you real supply chain visibility into service stability itself, not just a pin on a map. Track port order changes and ETA updates alongside the vessel's current position.

Step 2: Update your internal delivery time math using recent voyages, not just historical averages. Check whether transit has stretched or turned uneven over the last few weeks and adjust production and warehouse plans accordingly. Skip that, and delays at sea turn into shortages or downtime on land.

Step 3: Sync the downstream chain. Customs clearance, warehouse operations, and fleet scheduling all need to be ready for shifts in vessel timing, because even a small stretch in transit time raises demurrage and detention risk if nothing downstream adjusts.

Step 4: Get serious about transportation terms and insurance. If your route has already changed, or might, check coverage and contract terms before departure, before an incident forces the issue.
