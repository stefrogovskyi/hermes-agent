META-TITLE: Red Sea Reroutes 2026: Why Transit Times Jumped 40%

META-DESCRIPTION: Cape of Good Hope reroutes are adding 10-20 days per voyage and cutting capacity. See how Europe and the US are handling the 2026 shipping crunch.

BODY:

Ships crossing from Shanghai to Lisbon now spend the length of a fiscal quarter's planning cycle just getting there, five and a half weeks at sea, and that number alone tells you how far the map has been redrawn this year.

This isn't a story about one bad week at a canal. It's a structural rewiring of how goods move between Asia and Europe. Carriers, Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM among them, have pulled most of that traffic off the Suez Canal and out of the Red Sea, sending vessels around the Cape of Good Hope as the new default routing. Voyage distances have stretched accordingly, and the market is now absorbing an extra 10 to 20 days per trip. The bigger issue sits underneath that number: vessel cycle time is climbing, and every extra day a ship spends completing a rotation is a day it isn't available for the next one. Fewer completed cycles means fewer sailings on offer.

Here's what the March 2026 capacity picture actually looks like.

## How the Reroutes Are Eating Into Capacity

Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has effectively stopped. Insurance won't cover the risk anymore, security concerns are too high, and vessels are steering clear of the region altogether.

At the same time, the big carriers, Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, have suspended or rerouted their Suez and Red Sea services, funneling that traffic around the Cape of Good Hope instead.

What that's produced, in plain terms:
- transit times extended by 10-20+ days on key routes
- schedule reliability has fallen below 50%
- blank sailings have increased
- vessels are being offloaded at alternative ports due to route uncertainty
- congestion is building from network instability, and cargo volumes haven't actually grown to match it

Export flows out of India are already being described as choking under the combined weight of rising costs, rerouting, and corridors that no longer run predictably. Worth flagging: Hormuz rarely gets the same coverage as Suez in industry commentary, but its effect on available capacity is just as real.

## Europe Feels It Hardest

Europe is the region most exposed to disruption coming out of Asia. The real problem isn't the extra transit days themselves, it's that nobody can predict them anymore.

In practice, that looks like ETAs shifting several times over the life of a single shipment, containers arriving out of sequence, and cargo getting bumped onto later sailings on a routine basis.

### Operational impact
| Area | Impact |
| Retail | missed seasonal sales windows |
| Manufacturing | disrupted production cycles |
| Warehousing | uneven cargo inflow |

European shippers are responding in a few consistent ways. Buffer stock is up by 2 to 3 extra weeks across many companies trying to cushion against shortages and delays. Overbooking has become the norm. And dual routing, splitting cargo across sea and rail, is now a live strategy, no longer just a contingency plan sitting in a binder somewhere.

## The United States: Same Disruption, Different Playbook

The US is dealing with the same global disturbance, and its operational results look different.

East Coast routes show longer transit times as delays work their way through the system, yet the country's spread-out transportation network keeps things comparatively steadier. Cargo gets redistributed between West Coast and East Coast ports as needed, routing decisions run on real-time data, and inland transport gets leaned on to correct imbalances as they show up.

### Operational impact
| Area | Impact |
| Logistics | higher inland transport costs |
| Distribution | increased routing complexity |
| Inventory | higher carrying costs |
| Planning | shift to scenario-based execution |

Market behavior on the ground: mid-term freight contracts are gaining favor, port diversification strategies are spreading, and interest in nearshoring, particularly Mexico and the rest of Latin America, keeps climbing.

## Europe vs the US: Two Systems Absorbing the Same Shock Differently

The disruption is global. What each system can do about it depends on the constraints built into it long before 2026.

| Factor | Europe | United States |
| Main constraint | dependency on long-haul imports | routing flexibility |
| Cost structure | front-loaded (ocean freight) | distributed (ocean + inland) |
| Risk point | arrival timing | total logistics cost |
| Adjustment speed | slow (contract-driven) | fast (spot-driven) |

Europe's supply chains were built for efficiency over distance: heavy reliance on Asian imports, centralized distribution hubs, long planning cycles tied to fixed maritime schedules. That setup works beautifully when transit times hold steady. Small inconsistencies, even just 5 to 7 days off schedule, ripple out of proportion to their size. Warehouse planning breaks down, inbound flows stop lining up with outbound commitments, and contract penalties start piling up.

European companies are responding cautiously. Inventory is climbing, driven by uncertainty more than actual demand. Capacity is getting locked in earlier, even at a premium. Reliance on any single supplier or route is quietly getting dialed back. The shift underway is structural: Europe is trading efficiency for redundancy.

The US system was never built around one corridor to begin with. Multiple ocean gateways, substantial domestic redistribution capacity, and shorter decision cycles give companies more room to react fast when something breaks. The tradeoff shows up in cost: inland transport, rail and trucking, becomes the bottleneck, switching ports adds handling expense, and inventory gets repositioned more frequently than before. Budgets are shifting from procurement toward logistics, service reliability is getting prioritized over margin, and real-time visibility tools are doing more of the routing work than they used to. The US isn't trading efficiency for redundancy the way Europe is, it's trading efficiency for adaptability.

Strip away the geography and the real difference is where each system takes the hit. Europe absorbs it in time. The US absorbs it in money.

## Three Things Worth Tracking Beyond the Delays

Two chokepoints, the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea, are driving three measurable effects through the whole system right now.

Capacity is being pulled out of the market: the longer Cape routes are soaking up roughly 2.5 million TEU of global capacity. Flows are breaking down at the network level, with cargo getting offloaded at alternative ports and rerouted mid-journey. And costs have stopped being predictable, climbing on fuel, insurance, and congestion all at once.

Freight companies, cargo owners, and traders are adjusting accordingly. Cargo is getting staged across multiple regions to reduce exposure to any one chokepoint. Single-route logistics is giving way to multi-corridor strategies built with redundancy in mind. And higher costs are increasingly just the price of keeping supply moving at all.
