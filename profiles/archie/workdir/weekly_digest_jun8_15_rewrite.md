# Port Delays Are Linking Up Across Oceans

**Meta-title:** Weekly Logistics Digest: Ports, MSC, Customs
**Meta-description:** The week of June 8-15, 2026: MSC reshapes South Asia services, congestion builds between Europe and Asia, Savannah hits 55%, customs holds grow.

Week of June 8 to 15, 2026. Four stories moved freight markets this week, and they share one thread: a delay that starts in one place rarely stays there. Here is what happened and what it means for anyone trying to keep deliveries on schedule.

## MSC is rearranging South Asia

MSC is reportedly overhauling chunks of its South Asia carrier network and changing how its regional hubs run. If your cargo touches India, Sri Lanka, or Bangladesh, this can shift transit times, transshipment points, and vessel connections. Schedule reliability along those lanes may wobble for a while.

The practical part comes before any vessel sails. Network changes feed straight into booking decisions, cut-off planning, and the arrival windows you promise customers. A small hub adjustment upstream tends to ripple through everything downstream, so a shipment that relies on a connection deserves extra attention. When monitoring vessels on these routes, look at departures, the full routing path including intermediate ports, and how reliable the connections have actually been.

## Congestion between North Europe and East Asia has become a loop

Port congestion keeps topping the list of pressure points, and right now the delays between North Europe and East Asia are feeding each other. A ship leaves late, misses its slot at the next port, waits for a new berth, and every cargo riding the same rotation slips further back. Round it goes.

For shippers caught in that loop, the usual symptoms show up: cargo gets rolled, cut-offs slip by, transits stretch out, and demurrage or detention charges have more room to pile up.

Congestion numbers published once a week describe last week's problem. Live delay alerts tied to container tracking are what turn it into something you can act on while the vessel is still moving. That means adjusting plans for upcoming stages of the journey and telling customers where things stand based on real-time data rather than optimism.

## Savannah's Ocean Terminal is over halfway there

In the US, the Georgia Ports Authority reports that renovation of Ocean Terminal in Savannah has reached 55% completion. It sits inside a broader push to upgrade port infrastructure ahead of future cargo growth.

Nobody should expect a construction project to fix this month's delays. Expanded terminal capacity works on a different clock: better cargo handling room, space for mixed cargo flows, more resilience when peak season squeezes berths. If you ship through the US East Coast, track how this progresses anyway. Terminal development eventually shapes which ports make sense, which routing options exist, and how you plan capacity years out.

## Customs got stricter in North America

Enforcement around counterfeit and non-compliant goods has picked up across North America. More inspections mean more containers sitting in customs holds, more documentation reviews, and slower cargo releases.

Here is where location tracking alone runs out of answers. A container can be physically at the port and still weeks away from being releasable. What actually determines delivery is customs status, inspection risk, whether documentation is complete, and compliance questions that nobody flagged until the box was already on the quay. Forwarders and shippers who can see customs-related milestones, not just GPS dots, spend less time guessing and give customers answers that hold up.

## What this week adds up to

Delays used to arrive with a single cause attached. Increasingly they arrive tangled together: congestion plus a changed transshipment hub plus a missed connection plus an inspection hold. Discovering any of these after the fact is expensive; discovering them in real time is manageable.

Teams handling freight in 2026 are watching a wider set of signals than before:

- container location and vessel movement;
- port congestion and schedule reliability;
- transshipment delays;
- customs holds and release status;
- predictive ETA changes and risk alerts;
- inland delivery milestones.

Visibility started as a nice dashboard feature. It has become basic equipment for managing freight risk, keeping costs contained, and giving customers honest timelines. The companies that track shipments in real time and catch delay signals early will simply handle uncertain weeks like this one better than those waiting for status emails.
